On 7 June 1968, 187 sewing machinists – all of them women – walked out of the Ford Motor Company’s flagship plant in Dagenham, Essex. Less than two weeks later, they were joined by their fellow sewing machinists at the Ford Halewood plant on the other side of the country, doubling their numbers. Without the chairs they sewed, none of the thousands of cars that usually left the plants every month could leave the plant. 

They had taken the decision to strike after discovering that they had been classed as Category B (unskilled) in the new pay-grading system, which had been agreed by management and unions nine months before. This meant a 15% pay difference from their colleagues in Category C, and flew in the face of the machinists’ understanding of the skilled handiwork required for their job. They immediately understood that the company was attempting to underpay them based on their gender:

> _All the women were shouting the same thing: Just because we’re women, they think we can be left in Grade B. That's all they think of us!_

The only women not graded B were two ‘prototype sewing machinists’ in the R&D department. In their first grievance, the sewing machinists argued they should have been graded C ‘alongside their sisters’ in R&D. After months of meetings failed to come to any results, they called an unlimited overtime ban on 22 May, and threatened a one-day strike if they were not up-graded by 29 May. On that day, as the matter was once again being discussed by the company’s Profile Review Committee, a delegation of shop stewards interrupted the meeting to announce that the women would strike the next day if a satisfactory offer was not made. Even this level of militancy wasn’t enough for the sewing machinists. When the startled union convenors phoned the plant, they were told the women would walk out that afternoon.

As per company policy, a stern letter was sent warning the sewing machinists that their ‘unconstitutional action’ – taken without union or even stewards’ approval – was against their employment contracts, and calling them ‘anarchic and irresponsible’. Insulted, they immediately held a meeting, where they agreed to reaffirm their overtime ban and strike again in two weeks (7 June), this time indefinitely. Shop steward and leader Rosie Boland declared that they were prepared to strike ‘for a year if necessary’ and repeatedly raised the defiant slogan ‘we are not going back’.

By the end of June, Ford had run out of its seat reserves, shutting down car production, and was losing £1 million of revenue every day. The company appealed directly to the government. Such a confident ‘unconstitutional’ strike paralysing a key industrial giant was so concerning to Whitehall that PM Harold Wilson himself eventually called on Barbara Castle, Secretary of State for Employment, to find a way to get the sewing machinists back to work.

After Castle called the first of two courts of inquiry, the sewing machinists sent Wilson a letter, which was ‘urgently’ passed to the Press Office. It shows not only their self-assured heroism, but hints at their attitudes to unions, parliament, the government, the slogan ‘equal pay’ (though as we will soon see, this requires further discussion to interpret), and how they saw their strike as the first call to arms of a ‘great fight’ that would ‘soon’ involve workers in ‘every industry’:

Ultimately, the strike would end on 1 July, after three continuous weeks (the Halewood workers held out for a few more days). In negotiations with Castle, they had refused to go back to work before a settlement, but accepted a tempting offer that sidestepped their central demand on grading: an immediate raise to bring their pay up from 80% to 92% of the men’s, an industrial tribunal to rule on their grading, and legislation to “over a period of time, make equal pay a reality”, which would eventually become the Equal Pay Act 1970. The Court of Inquiry, as it turned out, did not rule on the grading system at all (which it sensibly understood would result in an immediate walkout). Instead, it delegated this to a committee of three union convenors, three management representatives, and an ‘independent’ chairman who voted with the company. You can imagine how this ended up after six weeks of deliberations. The machinists would not get their all-important up-grade to Category C _until 1984!_

Boland’s verdict shows the strikers’ dismayed and pyrrhic attitude to this ‘victory’: “although we did get more money, we did not gain the point, we won a battle, but lost the war”.

## A strike for equal pay, or equal pay against the strike?

The 40th anniversary of the Equal Pay Act in 2010 brought renewed interest in the strike – including a comedy-drama film, Made in Dagenham (2010) and a musical adaptation – which tended to focus on its supposed role in heralding legal pay equality as ‘a strike for equal pay’.

This prompted Sheila Cohen to publish ‘Equal pay – or what? Economics, politics and the 1968 Ford sewing machinists’ strike’ in the journal *Labor History*, arguing that although the strikers recognised that they were up against sexism, “the concrete, specific issue of grading – not ‘equal pay’ – was the key dynamic underlying the eventual walk-out” – as it was for many enraged male Ford workers who submitted hundreds of up-grading grievances and took up the slogan “equal pay for equal work”. The Halewood workers and Dagenham trim shop workers repeatedly asked for assurances that the strike was about pay grading, not equal pay for women, before joining the strike. Boland, although in favour of the principle of equal pay, emphasised that the basis of the strike was class, not gender: “there are so many men fighting for upgrading that if Ford's gave it to us, they would have to give it right through the firm”. Meanwhile, AEF representative (and former CPGB member, soon Maoist) Red Birch stated “we declare the strike official, but purely and solely in support of the principle of Equal Pay”.

Cohen's claim that “the sewing machinists’ placards at their union conference proclaiming ‘Equal Rights’ are probably the nearest the workers themselves got to a demand for equal pay” must obviously be amended in light of the letter, where ‘equal pay for women’ is named as their ‘great fight’. Clearly the idea had penetrated the strikers themselves, although it was not their cause at first. However, they understood this as a demand that could be the basis to bring out all workers, not just women workers, let alone the solution to discontent the government desired. Cohen’s argument that ‘equal pay’ served as a tool for the government, union officialdom, and management to redirect and defang the strike (shared with Huw Beynon, chronicler of Ford Haywood) is strong.

> _And what happened to this rank-and-file power, this unity, this determination? In marked contrast to the impression given by Made In Dagenham, it was punctured by a show of womanly solidarity from a female politician whose intentions were the opposite of her superlatively deceptive actions. Brought back into the fray on June 28 – the day after the failed Court of Inquiry – Barbara Castle was shown on the evening news daintily sharing a cup of tea and engaging in cosy feminine chat, laughing with the strikers’ representatives in ‘all girls together’ mode. Such personalised ‘intimacy’ was the last resort of a rattled ruling class – and it worked._

Not only did the feminist slogan of ‘equal pay’ lull the strikers into false solidarity with a female politician, it probably reduced their ability to appeal to male workers compared to a dispute focused on the grading system they too were fighting against. “Within the political enlargement of the issue, the central kernel of capitalist production relations and their impact on shop-floor experience was lost, if it had ever been recognised.”

Despite the painful cost of the women's pay rise, no-one gives better testimony to this than Ford themselves. Ford’s managing director described Castle’s ‘hair down’ approach as ‘brilliant’. Its Labour Relations Manager, after hearing of the settlement, donated £5 to the sewing machinists’ strike fund, declaring “I too believe in equal pay”!

## Self-organisation in action

What is remarkable in the Ford strike is the sheer determination, militancy, and self-initiative shown by the machinists, for many of whom this was their first ever strike (although they may have had experience supporting their male relatives). As Cohen eloquently portrays, the events were “rooted in… an… era of raw confrontation, workplace unionism described, even by shop stewards, as ‘jungle warfare’” (if it can even be called ‘unionism’, given that the unions had little role except to unsuccessfully urge the women back to work). From their first one-day strike, declared with no heed to the authority of the union convenors or shop stewards, to the final attempts of Castle to bribe the women to return to work to which Rosie Boland responded “That will be up to the girls whether they go back or not. I’d have to ask them first”, the rank-and-file sewing machinists exercised assertive democratic control over their own strike.

Until the last moment. As soon as the strikers put their faith in the government – a Labour government, no less, with a female Secretary of State for Employment – and left the initiative to the industrial tribunal, their strength was lost. This makes for a clear parallel to the [St Pancras Rent Strike](./stpancras/) on the other side of the decade.

It must be said that the ease of the authorities in bending the workers with clever tactics, despite their often sharp instincts (rejecting offers of negotiation in exchange for going back to work, scheduling a regular meeting after their one-day strike, etc) was helped by the strikers’ own underdeveloped political idea of what they did want, beyond being recognised as skilled workers, ‘equal pay’, and the basic class instinct that their fight involved ‘every industry’. It belongs to a category of strikes common at the time, which combined immense militancy with an outlook that did not go beyond winning immediate material improvements for a single sector (‘money militancy’). Although the sewing machinists won the admiration of their male colleagues, they could not hope to expand the scope of the strike – for instance, to dismantle the grading system entirely (a fight for which they appear to have understood their strike within) – without their active involvement. Closely related to their lack of experience, a more developed doctrine would've allowed them to look further and recognise Castle, the unions, and the court of inquiry for what they were. If the sewing machinists would not “politically enlarge” their strike, someone else would do it for them.
