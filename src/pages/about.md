*Strikin’ Sixties* was created as part of a digital history project during the author’s undergraduate studies in History, at the University of Leicester. It is intended not just to be an academic exercise, but a long-term public resource that can raise awareness of significant events that have been neglected and be used to learn about your own working-class history.
If you have any corrections, contributions, or just thoughts, please don’t be shy to contact the author at zhawker.aca@gmail.com.

## Viewpoint

History is not just the study of 'what really happened'. While there is a historical truth we’re trying to get as close as possible to, what sources a historian chooses to draw on and prioritise, how they weigh up opposing sources, and the methodology they used to weave them together, all depend on the historian's personal viewpoint. This is particularly the case for public history projects like this. For the public to be able to digest history easily, we have to curate a simplified narrative from the vast quantities of historical research (which is, unfortunately, often paywalled or inaccessible). *Strikin' Sixties* would obviously be very different if it focused on the perspectives of employers or government ministers!

How do we account for this unavoidable bias? On your part, you can approach this resource with critical eyes (if the content of this website isn’t enough to show you why that’s necessary!), seek out alternative viewpoints (some of which can be found in the further reading on each page), and do your own research. But digital history projects also create an opportunity for historians to put their viewpoints out in the open, as a visible, integral, and accountable part of their research. In the spirit of that, the author is happy to declare that their viewpoint in creating this project aligns with the perspectives of the Internationalist Communist Tendency ([leftcom.org](https://leftcom.org)) and the Marxist historical method.

In short, this means:
1. It's written from the perspective of workers first and foremost.
2. It views events as occuring within a class struggle between workers (who live by selling their labour for a wage) and bosses (who live off surplus value extracted from those workers). This class struggle links together workers in completely different sectors, or between struggles in the workplace and housing, or over racism and sexism, etc.
3. The state is viewed not as a neutral democratic body, but a collection of organs of the capitalist class for managing and maintaining their rule. This includes the government and parliament, but also the Labour Party, the Conservative Party, other parties which seek to run the capitalist state, the media, nationalised enterprises, and even the unions.
4. On that note, unions are properly understood as mediators between workers and capital, within that exploitative relationship. Of course, the workers inside unions are a very different question (as they themselves asserted!). All the behaviour of unions in this period cannot just be pushed under the rug as mistakes of an out-of-touch bureaucracy: they are a common pattern throughout the Twentieth Century and into today.
5. My interpretations of struggles look forwards to the possibility of breaking out of wage-labour, and see this as their hypothetical end-point. Part of my judgement is how much further the event/s brought us to this, while at the same time trying to bear in mind what the participants themselves (or even outsiders) viewed their goal as.
